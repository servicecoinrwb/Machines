Getting paid together, without a middleman holding it.

Four people play a gig. One of them collects, then spends a week chasing the others down. A church takes donations through a processor that keeps 2.9% and settles Tuesday. Someone subscribes to your newsletter and needs three emails to cancel.

All three are the same problem: money sitting somewhere it shouldn't, under rules only one side controls.

Three shapes, one thing

A till is a named pot with a link. What makes it a tip jar, a split, or a subscription is just how many payees it has and whether payments repeat.

Tip jar · one payee

The busker

A QR code in an open guitar case. Someone scans it, sends four dollars, and it's in your wallet before the next song. No account, no chargeback, no 30¢ minimum eating a small tip.

100%

Split · several payees

The four-piece band

Bar pays $850 for the night. It hits the till and divides on the way in — nobody holds anyone else's money, nobody Venmos anybody on Monday.

That last slice is worth noticing: a payee doesn't have to be a person. Ten percent to the van fund, or the PA, or a charity, taken off the top automatically instead of remembered later.

Plan · repeating

The monthly supporter

Five dollars a month to a newsletter. The supporter grants permission for a set amount, and the charge pulls against it on schedule.

Cancelling is taking the permission back. It happens in their own wallet, in one action, with nobody to talk to and no offer of two months free. That's not a policy anyone chose to be generous — the contract simply can't move money it wasn't permitted to move.

The reason it's that cheap is Arc: USDC is the network's own gas token, so the person paying doesn't have to hold anything except the dollars they're already sending.

What it won't do

It can't hold your money, on purpose. Every payment passes straight through to the payees in the same transaction. There's no balance in the contract, no withdraw button, and no admin key — which also means no escrow, no refunds, and no undo.

Payees are fixed when the till is created. Nobody can edit them afterward, including whoever made it. If the band's split changes, that's a new till. This is deliberate: a till whose payees could be edited is a till whose income could be redirected after the gig.

It can't force a charge. If a subscriber revokes permission or spends their balance, the charge fails and nothing happens. Nothing here chases anyone.

It's unaudited, and it's on a testnet. Every dollar you see is play money from a faucet. The contract is short, holds nothing, and its split math is verified against a reproducible simulation — but that's not a professional review, and Arc's mainnet hasn't launched.

Try it

Grab a dollar of test USDC from Circle's faucet, open a till with your own address as the only payee, and pay yourself. The whole loop takes about two minutes and costs less than a cent.
