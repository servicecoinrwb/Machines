The hard part was never the money. It's who decides the fact.

Building a betting pool is easy — take stakes from both sides, pay the winners. Every prediction market that has ever failed, failed at the next step: something has to declare what actually happened, and that something can be wrong, bribed, or simply absent.

So this contract doesn't hide the answer. It makes you pick a resolver before anyone stakes a dollar.

One pot, split by belief

There's no order book and nobody to match with. Both sides stake into a single pool, and whoever backed the correct outcome divides the whole thing in proportion to what they put in. The odds are just the shape of the pool.

Everyone on Yes splits the whole 350 pro rata. Someone who staked 100 of that 150 takes about 229 back — their money doubled and a half, because more people were wrong than right. If the pools had been reversed, so would the payout.

Because every payout is a division of money already deposited, the contract can never owe more than it holds. There is no leverage, no margin call and no scenario where it runs dry.

Two ways to settle it

Mode one

A price feed decides

The market names an oracle, a number and a comparison — above, below, at or above. After the close, anyone can settle it and the contract reads the answer itself. No human touches the outcome.

This is the better mode when it's available, and it only works for questions a feed can answer. It also refuses to settle from a stale feed: if the oracle has gone quiet for more than two hours, the transaction reverts rather than letting a frozen number decide.

Mode two

People decide, with money at stake

Anyone proposes the outcome and posts a bond. A challenge window opens. If nobody objects, the proposal stands and the bond comes back. If someone does object — posting a matching bond — the arbiter named at creation rules, and the losing bond pays them for the work.

The arbiter is public before the first stake. You're never asked to trust someone you didn't agree to.

  1. Open. Write the question, pick the resolver, set your fee.
  2. Stake. Both sides put money down. Odds move with every stake.
  3. Close. Staking ends. Nothing more goes in.
  4. Resolve. The feed answers, or someone proposes and the window runs.
  5. Claim. Winners collect their share; the creator's fee comes off the pot.

When it can't resolve, nobody loses

This is the clause worth reading twice. A market that gets stuck must not become a market nobody can exit.

What went wrongWhat happens
The arbiter never rulesAfter seven days anyone can void it. Every stake is refunded in full and both bonds go back — neither party is at fault for someone else's silence.
Only one side ever stakedVoided automatically. The "winners" didn't beat anyone, they guessed unopposed, so everyone gets their money back instead.
The price feed is frozenSettlement reverts rather than resolving on stale data.

No fee is taken on a voided market. The creator is paid for markets that resolve — not for markets they started.

What it isn't

It isn't a casino. There's no house, no edge, and no randomness anywhere in it. You're betting against other people's opinions about a fact, and the contract takes no side.

The arbiter is a real trust assumption. In human-resolved mode, a dishonest arbiter can rule wrongly and the bond mechanism only makes it expensive, not impossible. Read who it is before you stake.

Thin markets aren't informative. Two people with fifty dollars don't produce a probability, they produce a number that looks like one. Treat implied odds on a small pool as decoration.

Prediction markets are regulated in some places. Where real money and real events meet, this is a legal question and not only a technical one. Everything here is testnet play money.

Unaudited. The settlement math is verified against a simulation covering one-sided markets, voids, ties and four thousand randomized settlements with zero cases of payouts exceeding the pot. That isn't a professional review.
